Crochet Calculators

Two tools that do the arithmetic you would otherwise do wrong at eleven at night: your stitch and row counts, and how much yarn the project will actually eat.

Both of these start with a swatch

There is no way around it. Your gauge is personal to your hands, your hook and your yarn, and every number below is derived from it. A swatch takes fifteen minutes; the blanket that comes out the wrong size took three weeks.

Work a square at least 5 inches across in the yarn and stitch you plan to use, then measure 4 inches through the middle, away from the edges where the tension is irregular.

The chain figure is the number of working stitches. Add whatever turning chain your stitch needs on top — one extra for single crochet, two for half double, three for double crochet.

2. How much yarn

Most yardage charts guess from the project type. This works it out from the fabric you have actually made, which is the only figure that accounts for your tension, your stitch and your yarn at the same time.

Weigh your swatch on a kitchen scale — most read to 1 gram, which is close enough. Then take the length and weight printed on the ball band.

A 15 per cent margin is built into the figures above. That covers the edging, the swatch you sacrificed, and the two rows you will pull back. Buy the whole amount in one dye lot at the start — the same shade from a different lot will show as a band across the finished piece.

Where these calculators stop being accurate

  • A different stitch eats a different amount. Swatch in the stitch the project actually uses. Dense single crochet can take half as much again as an open double crochet over the same area.
  • Shaped pieces are not rectangles. For a garment or a round piece, add up the area of the rough rectangles it would fit inside, then treat the total as the project area.
  • Colourwork and stripes carry their own waste in carried floats and sewn ends. Add 10 per cent on top for anything with more than two colours.
  • Blocking changes the measurement. Block the swatch before measuring it if you intend to block the finished piece, which, in wool or cotton, you should.

Checking it against something known

A 50 × 60 inch throw in worsted weight is usually quoted at around 2,000 yards. Put a 4 × 4 inch worsted swatch weighing 5 g into the calculator above, with a 100 g / 220 yd ball, and it lands in the same place.

That is the test to apply to any yardage estimate, including this one: run it against a project whose answer you already know. If it disagrees badly, your swatch is not representative of the fabric you are about to make.
