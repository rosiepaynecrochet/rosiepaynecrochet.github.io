US vs UK Crochet Terms

The same abbreviation means two different stitches depending on which side of the Atlantic the pattern was written. Here is how to tell, and how to translate.

Why this one matters more than it looks

Both systems use the abbreviation dc. In the US it is a double crochet — three loops' worth of height. In the UK it is a double crochet too, but the UK stitch of that name is what the US calls a single crochet — the shortest stitch there is.

So the same two letters mean a tall stitch in one country and a short one in the other. Work a UK pattern as though it were US and every stitch comes out too tall, the fabric grows at the wrong rate, and the piece drifts further off size with every row.

Stitch translation

Read across. The US name and the UK name in the same row make the same stitch.

Slip stitch sl st Slip stitch ss
Single crochet sc Double crochet dc
Half double crochet hdc Half treble crochet htr
Double crochet dc Treble crochet tr
Treble crochet tr Double treble dtr
Double treble dtr Triple treble trtr
Triple treble trtr Quadruple treble qtr

The shaded rows are the two that cause the damage. Everything shifts by one step: whatever the pattern names, the UK stitch is one level taller in name than the US stitch it actually makes.

Working out which system a pattern uses

Most patterns say so at the top. Many free ones, especially older blog patterns and anything passed around as a photograph, do not. These checks settle it in a few seconds:

sc

Decisive — it is US

The UK system has no single crochet at all. If "sc" appears anywhere in the pattern, you are reading US terms. This is the fastest check and it never fails.

htr

Decisive — it is UK

Equally, "half treble" exists only in the UK system. The US equivalent is half double crochet, hdc. Either abbreviation settles it on its own.

tension

Strong hint — UK

UK patterns ask for tension where US patterns ask for gauge. Same thing, same swatch, different word.

miss

Strong hint — UK

"Miss the next stitch" is UK; "skip the next stitch" is US. Likewise UK patterns tend to write "yarn over hook" where US writes "yarn over".

DK · aran

Hint — UK or Australian

Yarn named by ply, or called DK or aran, leans UK. Yarn called worsted leans US. Not decisive on its own — both words travel.

mm only

Hint — UK or European

A hook given only in millimetres, with no letter, suggests a non-US author. US patterns almost always print the letter.

If none of the clues appear

Occasionally a pattern uses only dc and tr — both of which exist in both systems — and gives no other hint. Two ways to settle it:

  1. Check the stated gauge against the stitch. If the pattern says something like 14 stitches to 4 inches in dc on a 4 mm hook, that is a tall stitch, so dc means the US double crochet. A short stitch at that hook size would give closer to 18–20 stitches.
  2. Look at the photograph. Count the rows visible in a known length. US double crochet is roughly twice the height of UK double crochet, so a piece that looks open and airy at a modest row count is US dc.

If you are still unsure, work the first few rows in the taller reading. If the swatch comes out wider and shorter than the pattern claims, you have it backwards — and you have lost ten minutes rather than a week.

Translating as you go

If you are converting a UK pattern to work in US terms, the rule is mechanical: take each stitch name down one level. UK treble becomes US double crochet, UK double crochet becomes US single crochet, UK half treble becomes US half double.

UK tr   →  US dc
UK htr  →  US hdc
UK dc   →  US sc
UK ss   →  US sl st

Slip stitch is the one fixed point — it is the same stitch under both names. Mark up a printed copy before you start rather than translating row by row, because the row you mistranslate will be the one you do not notice until the piece is finished.
