Hook Size Conversion Chart

US letters, UK numbers and millimetres side by side — and why the millimetre is the only one of the three you can actually trust.

Read this before you trust any conversion chart

The millimetre is the only measurement that means the same thing everywhere. US letters and UK numbers are conventions, not standards, and manufacturers do not fully agree on them — a hook stamped G may be 4.0 mm from one brand and 4.25 mm from another.

So when a pattern gives you a letter, work from the millimetre beside it. When you buy, buy by millimetre. The letter is a nickname.

Three things this chart cannot tell you

13 → 2.25 mm

Bigger number, smaller hook

A UK 13 is 2.25 mm; a UK 3 is 6.5 mm. If a UK number looks large, the hook is small. US letters run the other way, A to S, small to large.

2.75 · 3.75

Gaps in the UK column

These two sizes exist in the US range with no UK counterpart. Charts that print a number here are guessing. Use the millimetre.

L — S

Where letters stop agreeing

Above about 8 mm, brands assign letters inconsistently. Two hooks both stamped N can be a millimetre apart, which is a whole gauge difference.

Steel hooks are a separate system — do not read them off this chart

Steel hooks, used for crochet thread, doilies and fine lace, carry their own numbering that runs opposite to everything above: the higher the number, the smaller the hook. A steel 14 is around 0.75 mm; a steel 00 is around 3.5 mm.

If a pattern calls for a "size 7 hook" and the project is a doily, it means a steel 7 (about 1.65 mm), not the 4.5 mm aluminium hook in the table. Thread weight is the clue: thread patterns name a thread number, yarn patterns name a yarn weight.

How to identify an unmarked hook

Inherited hooks and old sets often have worn-off markings. Two ways to work it out:

  1. Slide it through the holes of a knitting needle gauge — the hole it passes cleanly through, without wobbling, is its millimetre size. Measure the shaft, not the head.
  2. If you have no gauge, work a swatch and compare it against a hook you have identified. Same yarn, same tension — if the fabric matches, the hooks match.

Never judge by the head. Hook heads vary in shape between brands even at identical millimetre sizes, and an inline head makes a visibly different fabric from a tapered one at the same size.

Which hook for which yarn

Every yarn band prints a recommended hook, and it is a starting point rather than an instruction. The band assumes an average tension; yours is probably not average.

Go up a size if your fabric is stiff and curling, or if you want drape in a shawl or blanket. Go down a size if you can see gaps you did not intend, or if you are making amigurumi and the stuffing shows through.
