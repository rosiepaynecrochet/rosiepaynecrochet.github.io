Yarn Weights & Substitutions

Weights 0 to 7 with the hook and gauge each one expects — and the part most charts leave out: what to do when you cannot get the yarn the pattern names.

The eight weights

Gauge figures are single crochet stitches over 4 inches, the standard measure. Hook ranges are starting points, not rules.
No. US name UK / AU Hook Gauge Suits
0 Lace 2 ply, cobweb 1.5–2.25 mm 32–42 Doilies, fine shawls, thread work. Usually worked on steel hooks.
1 Super fine 3 ply, fingering, sock 2.25–3.5 mm 21–32 Socks, lightweight shawls, detailed amigurumi.
2 Fine 5 ply, sport, baby 3.5–4.5 mm 16–20 Baby garments, summer tops, anything that needs to stay light.
3 Light 8 ply, DK 4.5–5.5 mm 12–17 Garments and blankets where drape matters more than speed.
4 Medium 10 ply, aran, worsted 5.5–6.5 mm 11–14 The default for most patterns: hats, blankets, bags, amigurumi.
5 Bulky 12 ply, chunky 6.5–9 mm 8–11 Fast blankets, winter hats, scarves worked in a weekend.
6 Super bulky Super chunky, roving 9–15 mm 7–9 Chunky throws and cushions. Heavy — avoid for anything worn long.
7 Jumbo — 15 mm + 6 or fewer Arm and hand crochet, rug yarn, statement throws.

The number on the label is a category, not a specification

Two yarns can both be labelled 4 / medium / worsted and behave completely differently. The weight number describes a broad band of thickness, and the bands are wide — a number 4 covers everything from a lean worsted at 220 yards per 100 g to a dense aran at 160.

That difference is roughly a third more yarn per ball, which is the difference between buying six balls and buying nine.

Substituting one yarn for another

Almost every pattern names a yarn you cannot get, or can get but cannot afford. Substituting is normal. Doing it by weight number alone is where it goes wrong.

1. Match the yardage per gram, not the category

Every ball band prints a weight in grams and a length in yards or metres. Divide one by the other and you get the figure that actually describes the yarn's thickness:

yards per gram = yards on the band ÷ grams on the band

A yarn at 2.0 yd/g and a yarn at 1.6 yd/g are not interchangeable, whatever both labels say. Get within about 10 per cent of the original and the substitution will behave; beyond 15 per cent, expect to change hook size and re-do your gauge from scratch.

2. Buy by total length, then add a margin

Patterns often state "6 balls" rather than a yardage, which is meaningless once you change yarn. Convert first:

total yards needed = balls × yards per ball (of the original yarn)
balls to buy       = total yards ÷ yards per ball (of your yarn)

Then round up and add one. Running out four rows from the end, with the dye lot sold out, is the single most common way a finished project becomes an unfinished one.

3. Expect the fibre to change the result even at identical gauge

Matching gauge gets you the right size. It does not get you the same fabric.
Fibre Behaves like Substitute with care when
Wool Stretches and springs back. Blocks properly and holds the new shape. Warm, light for its bulk. Replacing it in a fitted hat or cuff — the elasticity is doing structural work that cotton cannot do.
Cotton No memory and no spring. Heavy, crisp, excellent stitch definition. Grows under its own weight. Used for anything hanging: a cotton version of a wool garment will lengthen and stay lengthened.
Acrylic Light, cheap, hard-wearing. Will not block — heat flattens it permanently rather than shaping it. The pattern depends on blocking to open up lace or flatten a square. Acrylic will not comply.
Wool / acrylic blend Most of wool's behaviour at acrylic's price and washability. The usual sensible compromise. Rarely a problem. The most forgiving substitute for a named wool yarn.
Bamboo / viscose Heavy, slippery, very fluid drape. Beautiful for shawls, structurally useless for bags. Anything that must hold a shape. It will drape whether or not you wanted drape.
Chenille / velvet Soft, but worms — loops push out of the fabric and will not go back. Substituting into any open stitch. These need a dense fabric and a small hook to stay put.

Holding strands together

If you cannot find the weight you need, you can often make it by holding two thinner strands as one. It is approximate, and it changes the texture, but it works:

  • Two strands of lace ≈ one fingering
  • Two strands of fingering ≈ one sport to DK
  • Two strands of DK ≈ one aran to bulky
  • Two strands of worsted ≈ one super bulky

Doubling never gives exactly double the thickness, because the strands flatten against each other. Always swatch — and buy the yarn in pairs of matching dye lots, since you will be pulling from two balls at once.

A substitution, worked through

A blanket pattern calls for 8 balls of a yarn at 100 g / 190 yd. The yarn you can actually buy is 100 g / 230 yd.

original: 190 ÷ 100 = 1.90 yd/g
yours   : 230 ÷ 100 = 2.30 yd/g  → 21% thinner

total needed: 8 × 190 = 1,520 yd
balls to buy: 1,520 ÷ 230 = 6.6 → buy 8

Six and a half balls covers the yardage, but the yarn is a fifth thinner, so your gauge will not match on the hook the pattern names. Drop a hook size, swatch, and expect to work more stitches for the same width — which eats yarn the arithmetic above did not account for. Hence buying eight, not seven.
